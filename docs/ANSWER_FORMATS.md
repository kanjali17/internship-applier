# Answer formats for internship free-response questions

How to answer each common essay type: what readers screen for, the shape that works, and length. Researched from company recruiting blogs and university career offices (sources at the end). Source key: **[A]** company recruiting team or university career office, **[B]** established prep outlet, **[C]** general career site, used only to confirm.

## Rules that apply to every box

- **Specific beats passionate.** Palantir recruiters ask: "Did the candidate do research and hone in on what specifically excites them about this opportunity in the short answer questions?" and "Is it obvious that the candidate took time and put thought into their answers?" [A] (Palantir, Pipeline to Prospect). They also say "we *do* read the optional responses" and flag rehearsed pitches, misspelled company names and untailored answers [A] (Palantir, How to get an internship).
- **Outcomes, not activities.** Google's recruiter formula: "Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z." [A] (CNBC / Google). Palantir wants "what you've actually done," not job descriptions [A].
- **Real, first-person material.** Anthropic: "create your first draft yourself, then use Claude to refine it"; do not submit "generic content with experiences you haven't actually had." [A]
- **Length:** write to about 80% of the limit. Unlimited box: 150-250 words. Stanford GSB's benchmark for a single example is 1,200 characters, about 200 words [A].

## 1. "Why [company]?" / "Why us?"

**Screened for:** a reason only true of this company; evidence you researched it; mission fit that is concrete. Vague lines like "I love AI" or "you're doing important work" tell the reader nothing, and leading with prestige or pay reads badly [B] (IGotAnOffer). Anthropic recruiters "want to hear that you've thought about why this company, not just why AI" [B] (Exponent).

**Structure (100-200 words):**
1. Hook from your own use of the product or a problem you hit (1-2 sentences).
2. One or two concrete reasons tied to a named product, technical choice or bet the company made.
3. Evidence you have already worked on that problem.
4. Close: what you want to build or learn there.

**Mistakes:** opening with "X's interns work on..."; mission-statement flattery; listing three generic reasons; praising the company for things every competitor also does.

**Template:** "I use [product] for [real task], and the part that caught my attention is [specific detail]; I built [related thing], so I want to work on [specific area] here."

## 2. "Why this role / team?" (incl. "Why product management?", "Why TPM?")

**Screened for:** that you understand what the job is day to day, and that your past choices already point toward it. Lenny's advice for aspiring PMs is to show "raw strengths" through things you built or led: "Find a small problem and try to solve it for someone" [B]. For TPM, the role is "influence, not authority": "You don't own the engineers. You don't own the product roadmap. But you're the one who keeps the program moving" [B] (Exponent TPM guide).

**Structure (100-200 words):**
1. The moment you noticed you gravitate to this kind of work (a real task, not "I've always loved").
2. Two pieces of evidence that you already do the job informally.
3. What the role adds that your current path lacks.
4. The specific team or problem at this company you want.

**Mistakes:** defining the role back to the reader; "PM is the CEO of the product"; claiming PM/TPM because you "like people and tech"; no evidence of already doing the work.

**Template:** "On [project] I kept ending up doing [role-specific work], which is when I realized I want to [core of role] full time, starting with [team/problem]."

## 3. "Project you're proud of" / "Most technically challenging project"

**Screened for:** your personal contribution, the hard part, the decisions and trade-offs, and a measurable result. Palantir looks for people who "took initiative to solve really hard problems" and explain why they invested time beyond coursework [A]. CMU says quantify results and focus on the steps you personally took [A].

**Structure (150-250 words):**
1. One sentence: what it is and who it is for.
2. The hard part, named precisely (not "it was complex").
3. Two decisions you made and why, including one trade-off or thing that did not work.
4. Result with a number.
5. Optional: what you would do next.

**Mistakes:** stack lists with no reasoning; "we" throughout so your part is invisible; describing features instead of the difficulty; no numbers; answering "proud" when they asked "challenging" (or vice versa).

**Template:** "I built [thing] so [users] could [outcome]; the hard part was [specific problem], which I solved by [decision] instead of [alternative], and it [metric]."

## 4. Challenge / failure / conflict

**Screened for:** ownership, judgment under pressure, and self-awareness. Palantir: "Don't sugarcoat your previous experiences... Acknowledging why it wasn't right for you demonstrates critical thinking, self-awareness" [A].

**Structure: STAR or CAR (150-250 words).** CMU's proportions: Situation 20%, Task 10%, Action 60%, Result 10% [A]. For failures add a one-line "what I changed afterward."

**Mistakes (CMU [A]):** blaming teammates or managers; no concrete result; overusing "I, I, I" when teamwork mattered, or hiding in "we" when it did not. Also: a humblebrag "failure," or a conflict with no real disagreement.

**Template:** "When [situation], I had to [task]; I [2-3 specific actions], which led to [result], and now I [lasting change]."

## 5. "How will your experiences make an impact here?" / "What would you bring?"

**Screened for:** a credible transfer from what you did to what they need. Readers want outcomes and fit, not a resume replay [A] (Palantir; UT Liberal Arts: "Describe your past experiences that demonstrate your ability to succeed in this role").

**Structure (100-200 words):** pick 2 strengths max. For each: claim, proof with a number, one line on where it applies at this company. Close with one specific first contribution.

**Mistakes:** listing every resume line; adjectives with no proof ("hardworking, passionate"); vague impact ("add value"); not naming anything at the company.

**Template:** "I bring [skill], proven by [result with number], which maps directly to [named team/problem] where I'd start by [concrete contribution]."

## 6. "What are you most excited to work on in your next internship?"

**Screened for:** direction and self-knowledge, and whether that direction matches their work. Palantir: "Show us what you care about. Explain what you're actually passionate about and why" [A].

**Structure (75-150 words):** the problem you want to work on; why it pulls you (tie to something you built); what you want to learn that you have not yet; how it fits this company.

**Mistakes:** "anything, I'm flexible"; listing technologies with no problem; describing a job they don't offer.

**Template:** "I want to work on [problem], because building [past project] showed me [insight], and I haven't yet [new skill] at [scale/context]."

## 7. Short cover letter / "Anything else you'd like us to know?"

**Screened for:** anything the resume cannot show: the why behind a line, context, a non-obvious fit. Stanford GSB frames its optional box as the "how" or "why" behind the "what," and says it is genuinely optional [A]. For engineering cover letters: "3 paragraphs maximum," ideally half a page; avoid restating the resume and templated "passionate and innovative" language [C] (techinterview.org). The Muse suggests adding an overlooked qualification or a brief fit summary, not nothing [B].

**Structure:**
- Cover letter (200-300 words): P1 why this role at this company, concrete. P2 one or two proof stories mapped to the role. P3 short close.
- "Anything else" (50-120 words): one new fact (a project not on the resume, a context note, a relevant skill), or leave blank if the application is truly complete.

**Mistakes:** repeating the resume; generic filler; a second "why us"; over-explaining.

**Template:** "One thing my resume doesn't show: [specific fact], which matters here because [link to role]."

## 8a. PM: "A product you love and how you'd improve it"

**Screened for:** product sense: a user segment, a real pain point, and a prioritized fix with trade-offs. Exponent: "Pick a product you actually use. Explain the user problem it solves, what it does well, and one improvement you'd make," and commit to one segment [B]. IGotAnOffer's BUS framework: Business objective, User problems, Solutions; and "Don't be afraid to poke holes in your favorite product even if it's made by the company you're interviewing for" [B].

**Structure (150-250 words):** what it is and its business goal (1 line); your segment and how you use it; what it does better than alternatives; one friction point you actually hit; one improvement, how you'd measure it, and the trade-off.

**Mistakes:** a feature wishlist; no segment; praising without critiquing; improvements that ignore the business model; picking an obscure product to seem clever.

**Template:** "For [segment], [product] wins because [reason]; the gap I hit is [friction], so I'd ship [fix], measure [metric], and accept [trade-off]."

## 8b. TPM: "A cross-functional project / managing ambiguity or dependencies"

**Screened for:** aligning teams with competing incentives, adapting when plans break, and proactive communication; strong candidates "create systems (like regular syncs, dashboards, or status reports) that reduce ambiguity" and lead with outcomes, since "the ability to be succinct" separates good from great [B] (Exponent TPM).

**Structure (150-250 words, STAR):** the goal and the teams involved; the dependency or ambiguity; the mechanism you created (plan, standard, checkpoint, doc); how you got buy-in without authority; outcome with a number.

**Mistakes:** long setup; "I followed up a lot" with no system; no stakeholder named; hero story where others were obstacles.

**Template:** "To ship [outcome] across [teams], I resolved [dependency] by creating [mechanism] and getting [stakeholder] to agree to [change], which delivered [metric]."

## 9. Short factual ones

- **"How familiar are you with us?"** (1-3 sentences). Name real products you have used and how. Palantir flags lack of research as a top mistake [A]. Template: "I've used [product] for [task] and read about [specific launch/feature]."
- **"How did you hear about us?"** One line, truthful: career fair, referral name, hackathon, product use. A referral name is worth including.
- **Salary expectations.** Give a researched range, not a single number, or write "Open / market rate for the role" if allowed. The Muse suggests a 10-15% spread with your target as the low end, or deferring until you know more [B]. Many posted intern ranges are public; use the posted range when one exists.
